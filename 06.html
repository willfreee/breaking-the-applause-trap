Breaking the Applause Trap

Chapter 6: But Doesn't Validation Feel Wonderful?

You might be thinking something right now.

I hear all of this. I understand the slot machine. I see the Magic Trick. I get it — the number isn't a mirror, the notification isn't oxygen, the whole thing is manufactured. But when someone validates me, it feels good. Really good. And if it feels good, how can it be an illusion?

That's a fair objection. And you're not wrong about the feeling.

I had the same objection for years. It felt so good — the compliment, the agreement, the notification — that I couldn't believe the feeling was part of the trap. But the feeling was never the problem. The meaning I attached to it was.

So let's talk about that feeling directly.

The Objection

Here's what you're telling me: You can explain the mechanics all you want. But the feeling is real. When someone likes my post, compliments my work, agrees with me — I feel good. That has to mean something.

And you're right. The feeling is real.

When the notification arrives, there's a warm flutter in the chest. A brief, undeniable sense of yes, I'm okay. It's pleasant. It's physical. It's not imaginary. You're not making it up. Something actually happens in your body when the validation lands.

I'm not going to tell you the feeling is fake. I'm not going to tell you to ignore it. I'm not going to tell you it doesn't matter.

But let me ask you something.

Does a real feeling always mean what you think it means?

If you drink salt water when you're dying of thirst, it feels wonderful for a moment. The liquid hits your throat. Your body responds. For a few seconds, the thirst seems to quiet. But the salt water isn't good for you. It doesn't hydrate you. In fact, it makes the dehydration worse. The feeling was real. The relief was real. But the meaning you attached to it — this is what I needed — was wrong.

The Sugar on the Sawdust

You remember the sawdust from Chapter 3. The bowl of useless filler that fills your stomach without nourishing your body. The substitute that mimics food without being food.

You might be thinking: But validation isn't sawdust. Sawdust doesn't feel good. Validation feels great.

Fair point. Let me adjust the metaphor.

Imagine sawdust coated in sugar.

Sweet. Pleasant. It goes down easy. It tastes like something real. For a moment, your body responds as if it's being fed. The taste buds light up. The stomach quiets. It feels like food.

But it's still sawdust.

The sugar makes the sawdust palatable. It makes it enjoyable. It makes you want more. But it doesn't make it nourishing. The pleasant taste is real. The nourishment is absent.

What if the "wonderful" feeling is the sugar coating?

What if the pleasure of validation is real — genuinely real, genuinely felt — but the substance underneath is still empty? The feeling is the sweetness. The nourishment is the lie.

The sawdust doesn't become food just because it's coated in sugar. And the approval doesn't become nourishment just because it feels good.

What the Relief Actually Means

Let's look closer at that relief.

The tight chest loosens when a response finally arrives. The hollow stomach settles when the number goes up. The restlessness quiets when someone agrees with you. The relief is undeniable. It feels like a problem was just solved.

But what was the problem?

The problem was that you were waiting. The problem was that you were checking. The problem was that the uncertainty was unbearable. The problem was the discomfort of not knowing — do they like me? am I okay? is this good enough?

The approval didn't solve a real problem. It ended the discomfort that the waiting itself created.

If I put tight shoes on you and then take them off, the removal feels wonderful. The relief is real. Your feet ache less. You can finally relax. But what was the problem the removal solved? The shoes I put on you. The relief is real. The problem was manufactured.

So what does the relief actually prove?

Only that the discomfort existed. Not that the approval was necessary.

The relief doesn't prove that validation is good for you. It proves that the waiting was painful. And the waiting was painful because the loop made it painful. The checking created the uncertainty. The uncertainty created the discomfort. And the relief arrived when the discomfort was temporarily suspended.

The relief is real. The problem was manufactured. That's the Magic Trick — applied directly to the feeling itself.

Real Warmth vs. Manufactured Relief

Now, you might be worried about something.

Are you saying I should never enjoy compliments? That I should never feel good when someone appreciates me? That sounds cold. That sounds like you're asking me to become detached.

I'm not.

Let's distinguish between two feelings that look similar but are not the same.

In the first scenario, a friend says something genuine about you. Specific. Personal. Connected to who you actually are. I really admire how you handled that situation. That was brave. Or: You always make me feel understood when we talk. You feel warm. Seen. Appreciated. And the warmth stays. It doesn't vanish in thirty minutes. It lingers. It feeds something real.

In the second scenario, a stranger likes your post. A number goes up. You feel a brief spike. A flutter. A moment of yes. And then it fades. Within the hour, the emptiness returns. The number is higher, but the feeling is gone. And you're already checking again.

The first is real warmth. Real hunger being fed. The second is the sugar on the sawdust. The craving being temporarily quieted.

The book has no argument with the warmth. The warmth is genuine connection. The warmth is belonging. The warmth is one of the real hungers we talked about in Chapter 3. It feeds you. It lasts. It matters.

The book has an argument with the spike. The spike is not connection. It's not belonging. It's the slot machine paying out. It's the cord loosening. It's the manufactured relief of a manufactured problem.

Can you feel the difference?

The warmth is specific. The spike is abstract. The warmth is personal. The spike is anonymous. The warmth says I see you. The spike says the number went up. And those are not the same message.

So no, I'm not asking you to stop enjoying genuine appreciation. I'm not asking you to become cold. I'm asking you to stop mistaking the spike for the warmth. To stop calling sawdust food because it's coated in sugar.

When the Feeling Is a Warning Sign

And now, the final flip.

But the feeling is so good, you say. It can't be bad for me. Pleasure is a signal. It's telling me something real.

What if it is telling you something real?

But not what you think.

The "wonderful" feeling of validation is not evidence that validation is good. It's evidence that the craving was present. The intensity of the relief is directly proportional to the intensity of the manufactured discomfort.

Think about that. The tighter the cord, the better the loosening feels. The deeper the manufactured emptiness, the more "wonderful" the validation seems. The more unbearable the waiting, the more euphoric the arrival.

The pleasure isn't a sign of health. It's a sign of how much suffering the loop has been manufacturing underneath.

What if the "wonderful" feeling is actually a warning sign? Not proof that approval is good, but proof that the craving has become deep enough to make a temporary reprieve feel like salvation?

The more wonderful the relief, the more manufactured the suffering. The pleasure is real. But it's the pleasure of a problem temporarily suspended — not a need genuinely met.

That's the full dismantling of the Relief Illusion. The last of the seven faces of the Big Monster.

Validation feels wonderful, so pursuing it must be good for me.

The feeling is real. The conclusion is false.

The "wonderful" feeling was never proof that approval nourished you. It was proof of how deeply the loop had been starving you. The pleasure is real. The nourishment isn't.

And once you see that, the feeling starts to change. Not because you suppressed it. Not because you forced yourself to stop enjoying it. But because you can no longer mistake it for what it isn't.

The sugar still tastes sweet. But now you know it's sugar on sawdust. And sawdust, no matter how sweet, is still sawdust.

So the relief isn't proof that approval is good. It's proof that the craving was manufactured.

But there's another side to this coin.

Because if the relief is manufactured, what about the fear? What about the dread that arrives when the validation is absent? What about the cold tightening when the phone stays quiet, when the room goes silent, when someone disagrees?

That's the next chapter. Because the loop doesn't just sell you the sugar. It also sells you the fear of what happens when the sugar stops.

For now? Keep observing. Keep noticing the difference between the warmth and the spike. Keep watching the relief while it happens — and asking yourself: what problem was just solved? And who created it?
