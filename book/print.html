Breaking the Applause Trap

Chapter 1: Welcome to the Performance

You know that feeling.

The social gathering that should have been fine. The dinner with friends, the office happy hour, the family get-together. You went, you smiled, you talked, you laughed in all the right places. And when it was over, you got in your car or closed your front door and felt something you couldn't quite name. Not tired in the normal way. Not just "drained from being around people." Something deeper. A hollow, scraped-out exhaustion, like you'd just finished a shift you didn't remember signing up for.

And then the replay started.

Did I talk too much? Did I sound weird when I told that story? She looked away for a second — was she annoyed? Why did I laugh at that joke? I don't even think it was funny. I should have said something smarter. I shouldn't have said anything at all.

Three hours at a party. Three more hours in your head afterward. And for what?

I used to think that exhaustion was just the price of being a decent person. The cost of caring. The weight of being "good with people." I was wrong.

It was the cost of performing.

The Exhaustion You Can't Name

Here's a simple question. If socializing is supposed to fill you up — and for most of us, that's what we've been told — why does it so often leave you empty?

You might already have an answer ready. I'm just an introvert. That's a common one. I just get drained by people. Or the most popular: Everyone feels tired after socializing. That's normal.

Fair enough. But let's look closer.

When you're genuinely connecting with someone — really connecting, lost in conversation, not watching yourself from the outside — do you feel drained afterward? Think about the last time you had a conversation that actually energized you. The one where you lost track of time. The one where you said exactly what you meant without editing it first. The one where you weren't monitoring the other person's face for signs of disapproval every ten seconds.

That didn't leave you exhausted, did it? It left you full.

So the exhaustion isn't from socializing. It isn't from being around people. It's from something else entirely.

What exactly were you doing for those three hours that cost so much energy?

You were managing. Monitoring. Adjusting. You were tracking their moods, reading their reactions, modulating your tone, softening your opinions, laughing at jokes you didn't find funny, agreeing with points you didn't actually believe. You were editing yourself in real time, sentence by sentence, gesture by gesture, trying to make sure that every word out of your mouth landed the right way.

You weren't connecting. You were performing.

And performing is exhausting work. It requires constant vigilance. Constant self-surveillance. You're never present, because you're always watching yourself from the outside, checking the audience's reaction, adjusting the act.

That's the first thing I want you to see. Not as a criticism. As a recognition. The exhaustion you've been carrying isn't a personality flaw. It isn't introversion. It isn't the unavoidable cost of being a decent, social person. It's the cost of wearing a costume you never consciously chose to put on.

And here's the part that might surprise you: you're not the only one wearing it.

The Costume You Forgot You Were Wearing

I remember rereading a text message four times before sending it. It was a simple response to a simple request. The first draft said, "No problem." Then I changed it to "Of course!" Then I added an exclamation point and a smiley face. Then I deleted the smiley face because it felt desperate. Then I added it back because deleting it felt cold. Four edits. For a two-word reply. And the whole time, my heart was doing something subtle but unmistakable — a tight little flutter of dread, like the stakes of this text message were somehow enormous.

I thought I was the only one who did that.

I wasn't. And neither are you.

What if the thing you think is your unique brokenness is actually a shared, predictable pattern? What if, right now, in this exact moment, thousands of people are doing the exact same four-edit text rewrite, all convinced they're the only ones? What if the over-thinking, the over-explaining, the anxious replay loop — what if all of it is not evidence that you're uniquely flawed, but evidence that you're caught in something?

A trap.

Now, I know the word "trap" might feel strong. You might even resist it. I don't have a problem, you might be thinking. I just care about people. I'm just considerate. This isn't an addiction; I'm just a thoughtful person.

I hear you. But let me ask you something.

Think about the last time you said "yes" to something you didn't want to do. A favor. An invitation. A request that made your stomach sink the moment it was asked. Did you pause? Did you weigh the options and make a choice? Or did the word just come out of you — automatic, reflexive, almost before you knew it was happening?

And then, two seconds later, the dread arrived. That heavy wave in your stomach. Why did I say yes? I don't have time for this. I don't want to do this. Why can't I ever just say no?

Two seconds. That's the gap.

If saying "yes" were just kindness, where does the dread come from? Kindness doesn't produce dread. Generosity doesn't leave you feeling trapped and resentful. But compulsion does.

Or think about your phone. That little phantom check. You reach for it without deciding to. You refresh. You see no new notification. And for a fraction of a second, you feel it — a tiny deflation. A small sinking. Nothing dramatic. Just a faint, almost imperceptible signal that something is missing. Then you put the phone down. And thirty seconds later, you check again. As if something might have changed. As if the absence of a response might have somehow become a response.

That's not behavior you're choosing. That's behavior happening to you.

And that's the line. The line between caring and compulsion. Between genuine connection and the Applause Trap.

The Applause Trap

Let me be clear about what this book is — and what it isn't.

This book is not an argument against caring about people. It is not a case for becoming cold, detached, or indifferent. It is not going to tell you to stop enjoying compliments, to stop appreciating mutual affection, or to stop being a kind, considerate human being. None of that is the problem.

The problem is compulsion. The problem is the loss of choice.

Here's the difference in action. Imagine two scenarios.

In the first, a friend says something kind to you. A genuine compliment. You feel a natural warmth. You appreciate it. You say thank you. And then the moment passes, and you both move on. That's connection. That's real hunger being fed.

Now imagine the second scenario. The same friend says the same kind thing. But this time, something different happens inside you. You feel a brief rush of relief — like you've just been handed something you were desperate for. And then, almost immediately, the urge arrives: Keep it going. Say something agreeable. Don't lose this. Check if they still like you. Replay the moment and wonder if it was enough.

The first scenario is freedom. The second is the trap.

The difference isn't whether you care. It's whether you choose.

In the trap, you don't choose. You respond. Automatically. Reflexively. The "yes" comes out before you've decided. The text gets rewritten four times before you've noticed you're doing it. The phone gets checked before you've formed the intention to check it. The opinion gets softened before you've registered that you had one. The performance starts before the curtain even goes up.

That's what the Applause Trap is. It's the compulsive, automatic, ongoing attempt to manage what other people think of you — through seeking approval, avoiding disapproval, and constantly measuring yourself against everyone else. And it operates on a simple, brutal premise:

Your worth depends on their opinion.

That's the lie. That's the engine. Everything else — the people-pleasing, the comparison, the validation-seeking — is just the machinery built on top of that one false belief. And here's the crucial thing: the Applause Trap is not who you are. It's something you do. Something you learned. Something you were trained into. And because it's learned, it can be unlearned.

But not through willpower. Not through forcing yourself to stop. Not through white-knuckle discipline or boundary scripts or confidence hacks.

Through something far simpler. And far more counterintuitive.

Don't Stop. Just Watch.

I'm going to ask you to do something that might surprise you.

Don't change anything yet.

Not yet. Not while you're reading this book.

Keep people-pleasing. Keep checking your phone. Keep comparing yourself to other people. Keep performing. Keep managing. Keep doing exactly what you've been doing.

I mean it.

This is the exact opposite of what every self-help book, every productivity guru, every well-meaning friend has ever told you. They all say the same thing: Stop it. Change now. Just say no. Set a boundary. Put the phone down. Be more confident. And how well has that worked for you so far?

Have you ever tried to force yourself to stop caring what people think? Really tried? White-knuckled your way through a social situation, telling yourself over and over, I don't care, I don't care, I don't care? How did that go?

Let me guess. The more you tried not to care, the more you cared. The more you tried to stop overthinking, the more you overthought. The more you tried to be "authentic," the more performative you felt.

That's not a personal failure. That's a predictable result. Because the struggle against the trap is the fuel the trap runs on.

So here's the paradox: stop trying to stop. Don't fight the compulsion. Don't resist the urge. Don't try to change the behavior. Instead, just watch it.

Observe it. Notice it. While it's happening.

When you catch yourself rewriting that text message for the fourth time, don't stop. Let yourself rewrite it. But notice that you're doing it. When the "yes" comes out automatically and the dread arrives two seconds later, don't beat yourself up. Just notice the sequence. When you reach for your phone for the third time in five minutes, let yourself reach. But see your hand moving. See the impulse. See the tiny deflation when there's nothing there.

You don't have to stop the trick. You just have to see it.

Because here's what happens when you start to see it: the trick loses its power.

Think about a magician. A good one. You're watching the show, and the rabbit disappears from the hat, and for a moment you're genuinely amazed. You can't figure out how it happened. The illusion holds.

Now imagine watching the same trick, but this time you know how it's done. You've seen the hidden compartment. You know exactly where the rabbit goes. Does the trick still work? Technically, yes. The rabbit still disappears. But does it fool you? Does it produce that same gasp of wonder? No. Because you can see the mechanics.

That's what this book does. It shows you the mechanics.

You keep performing. But now you watch yourself perform. You see the hidden compartments. You see the strings. You see the machinery. And the more you see, the less power the performance has over you.

That's the Paradoxical Instruction. And it's not a delay tactic. It's the method itself.

The reason you don't need to force yourself to stop is that once you truly see what's happening — once the illusion breaks — the compulsion loses its grip on its own. Not because you wrestled it into submission. Because it simply stops making sense.

The Flutter Is Not the Enemy

Now, you might be thinking something. And it's a fair thought.

Even if I understand all of this, I'll still feel the anxiety. Understanding won't change how my body reacts. When that message goes unanswered, my chest is still going to tighten. When I see someone else succeeding, that hollow feeling is still going to show up. Knowing it's an illusion won't make the physical sensation go away.

I'm not going to tell you the physical sensation is imaginary. It's real. That tight chest, that hollow stomach, that quickened heartbeat — when a message goes unread, when a post plateaus, when someone disagrees with you in a group setting. That flutter is real.

But here's what I want you to know: it's small.

It feels big in the moment, but it's actually just a reflex. A normal biological system reacting to an engineered social fear signal. It's not a monster you have to fight. It's more like a coat on a chair in a dark room.

Have you ever been afraid of something in the dark? Really afraid? Your heart pounding, your breath quickening, every nerve in your body telling you something is there? And then you turn on the light, and it's a coat on a chair. And the fear just... goes. Not gradually. Not through effort. Instantly. Because the fear wasn't caused by the coat. It was caused by the dark.

The flutter you feel when validation is missing is the same. It's not caused by the missing validation. It's caused by the dark — by the false belief that you need the validation to be safe. And when the light comes on, the flutter starves.

So don't worry about the flutter. Don't try to suppress it. Don't try to fight it. Just watch it. It's the smallest part of this whole thing. The real work — the 99% — is in seeing the beliefs behind it. The beliefs that whisper:

Your worth depends on their opinion.

That's the lie we're going to dismantle together, piece by piece, chapter by chapter. And you don't have to do anything yet except keep your eyes open.

What You're Really Hungry For

Before we move on, I want to leave you with one more distinction. Because it will matter throughout this entire book.

There's a difference between what you're craving and what you're actually hungry for.

The craving is for approval. For likes. For head-nods. For surface compliments. For performative agreement. For the brief, flickering relief that comes when someone validates you. That's what the Applause Trap feeds you. That's what the compulsion chases.

But that's not what you're actually hungry for.

What you're actually hungry for is belonging. Self-trust. The ability to speak your truth without editing it. The capacity to sit in a room full of people and not perform. The deep, settled peace of knowing that you are enough without anyone having to confirm it.

That's real hunger. And here's the thing about real hunger: external validation never satisfies it. Not once. Not ever. It only mutes your awareness of the hunger temporarily. It's like eating sawdust when you're starving. It fills your stomach for a moment, but it gives you nothing your body can use. And an hour later, you're hungrier than before.

That's why the relief you feel when someone approves of you is so brief. That's why the emptiness comes back so quickly. That's why you have to keep seeking, keep checking, keep performing. Not because the approval is feeding you. Because it isn't.

The good news — and this is the good news — is that real hunger can be satisfied. Once the craving is seen for what it is, once the trap is dismantled, the real hunger can finally be fed. By genuine connection. By self-trust. By authentic expression. By the simple, quiet act of being yourself without asking permission first.

That's where we're going. But we're not there yet.

For now, all I'm asking is this: keep performing. Keep people-pleasing. Keep checking. Keep comparing. Don't try to change anything. Just start watching.

Because in the next chapter, we're going to look at the exact machinery of the trap — the loop that runs underneath all of it. The loop that turns comparison into inferiority, inferiority into validation-seeking, validation-seeking into people-pleasing, and people-pleasing into a temporary, hollow reassurance that always, always leads back to comparison.

Once you see the loop, you'll understand why the performance never actually delivers what it promises. Why the applause never lasts. Why the safety you're chasing keeps slipping through your fingers.

And for now? Keep your costume on. Keep your eyes open.

The show is about to be explained.

Chapter 2: The Loop: Compare, Perform, Repeat

You're not at the beginning of this story.

You're already somewhere in the middle. Again.

Maybe you just posted something and you're waiting. Maybe you just saw someone else's announcement — the new job, the new relationship, the new milestone — and something tightened in your chest. Maybe you just said "yes" to something you didn't want to do, and the dread is already settling in.

Wherever you are right now, I can tell you this with certainty: it's not the first time you've been here. And it won't be the last. Unless you see what's actually happening.

I used to think I had three problems. The comparing. The seeking. The pleasing. Three separate issues, three separate fixes, three separate battles to fight. I was wrong.

I had one problem. Running on a loop.

The First Station: Compare

It starts so quietly you don't even notice it starting.

You're scrolling. You're not looking for anything in particular. You're just... looking. And then your eyes land on something. A photo. An announcement. A milestone. Someone you know — or maybe someone you barely know — posting something that makes your stomach drop half an inch.

A peer got the promotion you wanted. A friend posted a photo from a party you weren't invited to. An acquaintance announced their engagement. A stranger shared their morning routine, and somehow it makes your entire life feel disorganized.

I'm just observant, you tell yourself. I notice what other people are doing. That's normal. It's just information.

But look closer at what actually happened in that moment.

You weren't just noticing. You were measuring. The instant your eyes landed on that post, something inside you ran a calculation. What does this say about me? Am I ahead or behind? Am I winning or losing? Am I doing life right?

That's not observation. That's evaluation. And evaluation always produces a verdict.

The verdict might not be conscious. It might just be a feeling — that dark tightening in the chest, that subtle sinking in the stomach. But it's there. And it's not neutral.

Comparison is never neutral. It can't be. Because the moment you compare, you're not gathering information about the world. You're gathering information about your position in it. And the filter you're running everything through is always the same:

Am I enough?

So the first station of the loop isn't something you decide to enter. It's something that happens to you. The scroll, the glance, the fleeting look — and the comparison is already running. The measurement is already happening. The verdict is already being prepared.

And the verdict, almost always, is the same.

The Second Station: Feel Inferior

Where did that feeling come from?

One minute you were fine. Scrolling, maybe a little bored, but fine. The next minute, something shifted. A quiet deflation. A sudden drop in mood. A low hum of not-enough-ness that wasn't there before.

You try to shake it off. I'm happy for them. I'm not jealous. I'm fine.

But the feeling doesn't lift. It settles. It makes itself comfortable. And now you're carrying something you weren't carrying two minutes ago.

Here's the question I want you to sit with: did something change about your life in those two seconds?

Did you lose something? Did you fail at something? Did someone tell you you weren't good enough? No. Nothing in your actual circumstances changed. Your job is the same. Your relationships are the same. Your bank account is the same. Your body is the same. Nothing out there moved.

But something in here moved. Something internal shifted. And that shift — that sudden drop — wasn't caused by a change in your circumstances. It was caused by the comparison itself.

But I'm not comparing myself to them, you might be thinking. I'm just inspired by them. Or maybe I'm just being realistic about my own shortcomings.

Are you?

When you see someone else's highlight and feel that tightening in your chest, is that inspiration? Does inspiration feel like a weight? Does realism arrive as a sudden, physical sinking? No. Inspiration lifts. Realism, even the hard kind, clarifies. But this feeling — this specific feeling — does neither. It deflates. It diminishes. It tells you, without words, that you are behind. That you are lacking. That you are somehow less.

That's not an honest self-assessment. That's the loop doing its work.

And here's the crucial thing: the loop doesn't just produce this feeling randomly. It produces it because the feeling is useful to the loop. Because once you feel inferior, you need to do something about it. The discomfort demands action. The hole demands filling.

And the loop has exactly the thing to fill it with.

The Third Station: Seek Validation

The impulse arrives so naturally you barely recognize it as an impulse.

You want to feel better. That's all. The comparison made you feel small, and now you want to feel okay again. So you reach for something. You check your own posts. You think about sharing something — a photo, a thought, an achievement. Not because you particularly wanted to share it before. But because right now, in this moment, you need a counterweight.

I just want to feel better, you tell yourself. Getting some positive feedback — that's healthy, right? That's self-care.

But what are you actually seeking right now?

You're not looking for connection. You're not reaching out to someone because you genuinely want to talk. You're looking for evidence. Evidence that you're okay. Evidence that you're enough. Evidence that the comparison was wrong about you.

And if you were already sure you were okay, would you need evidence? If you were already certain you were enough, would you be searching for proof?

Think about what that search feels like. The waiting. The refresh. The check. The tiny deflation when the number doesn't move. The hope that spikes when you see a notification, then collapses when it's not what you wanted. This isn't a pleasant social interaction. It's a desperate attempt to fill a hole that didn't exist three minutes ago.

That's the key insight. The hole wasn't there before the comparison. It was dug by the comparison. And now the loop is offering you the shovel to fill it back in — except the shovel is just another version of the same hole.

Validation-seeking is not a neutral social behavior. It's not "just wanting to be liked." It's a specific, targeted attempt to get external proof that the internal verdict of inferiority was wrong. And here's the trap: even if you get that proof, it won't last. Because the loop isn't designed to let it last.

The Fourth Station: People-Please to Maintain It

Let's say you get the validation. Someone likes the post. Someone agrees with you. Someone offers a compliment, a nod, a moment of approval. And for a moment, you feel it. The relief. The lift. The sense that maybe you're okay after all.

And then something strange happens.

Instead of relaxing into it, you start protecting it. The approval you just received suddenly feels fragile. Conditional. Under threat. So you start managing. You say "yes" to something you don't want to do, because the person who just validated you asked. You soften your opinion mid-conversation because a dominant voice disagreed. You reread the text message four times before sending it, because what if the wrong tone destroys everything?

I need to make sure it doesn't go away, you tell yourself. I need to protect this.

But think about what you're actually doing. You just got the validation you were seeking. Shouldn't that be enough? Shouldn't the relief last? Why do you immediately need to manage it, maintain it, defend it?

What would happen if you just... let it be there? Without managing it? Without protecting it? Without performing to keep it?

The answer, of course, is that you don't know. Because you've never tried. The moment approval arrives, the loop snaps into its next phase — the maintenance phase — and you're already performing again before you've even enjoyed the relief you worked so hard to get.

This is where the Performance Costume comes in. The edited version of you. The version that laughs at jokes it doesn't find funny. The version that agrees with points it doesn't believe. The version that says "yes" when it means "no" and apologizes for having preferences at all. The costume goes on automatically, because the approval you just received feels like something you have to keep earning.

But here's what the loop doesn't want you to notice: the approval was never yours to keep in the first place. It was always temporary. That's the next station.

The Fifth Station: Temporary Reassurance

The relief is real. I'm not going to tell you it isn't.

When the notification arrives, when someone agrees with you, when the compliment lands — you feel it. A warm flutter. A brief sense of safety. A moment where the tightness eases and you can breathe.

See? you tell yourself. Their approval is exactly what I needed. This proves it.

Does it?

If the approval were truly feeding you, why does the hunger return so quickly? If the reassurance were real, why does it fade? If the validation actually cured the insecurity, why are you back in the same spot thirty minutes later?

The answer is uncomfortable, but it's also liberating. The relief isn't evidence that the approval cured your insecurity. It's evidence that the insecurity was manufactured in the first place.

Think of a magician. A good one doesn't just perform tricks. A good magician stages a problem — the card is lost, the coin has vanished, the lady is trapped in the box — and then takes credit for solving it. The relief the audience feels when the coin reappears is real. But the problem the coin reappearance solved wasn't real. It was staged. Created. Manufactured by the trick itself.

That's what the loop does. It creates the feeling of inferiority through comparison. It creates the hole. It creates the anxiety. And then it offers you the solution: Go get some approval. That will fix it. And when the approval arrives, you feel relief. Real relief. But you're not relieved because a real problem was solved. You're relieved because a manufactured problem was temporarily suspended.

The Magic Trick. That's what this station is. And it's the reason the relief never lasts. Because the loop needs you to keep running. It needs the relief to fade so the comparison can start again. It needs the hunger to return so the search can resume. A satisfied customer stops buying. A relieved performer stops performing. So the loop makes sure the relief is always temporary.

The Sixth Station: Compare Again

And so the reassurance fades.

Not all at once. Slowly. Quietly. The warmth cools. The safety thins. And before you know it, you're back at your phone. Back on the feed. Back measuring yourself against people you don't even know.

The same trigger. The same tightening. The same feeling of not-enough-ness. The same search for evidence. The same performance to protect it. The same fleeting relief. The same fading.

Each time I go through this, I must be making progress, you tell yourself. At least I'm trying. At least I'm aware of it.

But look at the loop honestly. Where does it go? What does it produce? Does the tenth cycle feel different from the hundredth? Does the relief last longer? Does the comparison hurt less?

No. The loop doesn't progress. It doesn't lead anywhere. It just returns to the start. Like a hamster wheel, the only thing the running produces is more running. The only thing the loop generates is the energy to keep the loop going.

And here's the thing that makes it so hard to see: the loop is invisible while it's running. You don't experience it as a loop. You experience it as a series of separate moments — comparison, insecurity, seeking, pleasing, relief. Each one feels like its own thing. Each one feels like something you need to deal with separately.

But they're not separate. They're connected. Each one feeds the next. Each one is a station on the same track. And the train only stops when you see the whole thing.

One Mechanism, Not Three Problems

You've probably tried to fix this before. Most people have.

The book on confidence. The podcast on boundaries. The article on comparison. The video on people-pleasing. The self-help shelf in your mind, stacked with separate resources for separate problems.

I need to work on my comparison habit, you tell yourself. Then I need to deal with the validation-seeking. Then the people-pleasing. Three separate fixes. Three separate plans.

But what if there aren't three problems? What if there's one mechanism with three stations?

You don't fix a loop by working on each station separately. You don't solve a hamster wheel by trying to run slower in one section. You don't escape a closed circuit by redecorating each stop.

You escape by seeing the whole loop. Because once you see it, you can step off.

That's what this book is doing. Not giving you three separate fixes for three separate problems. Showing you the one mechanism that's been running underneath all of it. The loop that turns comparison into inferiority, inferiority into validation-seeking, validation-seeking into people-pleasing, and people-pleasing into a temporary reassurance that always, always leads back to comparison.

The loop's greatest trick isn't that it runs without you noticing. It's that it convinces you it's three separate problems so you keep treating symptoms while the engine keeps running underneath.

Now that you can see the loop, a question might be forming. What is it actually chasing? What is this whole machine trying to get? What is it that you're really hungry for when you reach for the validation, the approval, the applause?

That's the next chapter. Because the loop isn't chasing something you actually need. It's chasing a substitute. And once you see the difference between the substitute and the real thing, the whole mechanism starts to come apart.

For now? Keep watching. Keep the loop in view. Don't try to stop it. Don't try to change it. Just observe it while it runs — the same Paradoxical Instruction from Chapter 1. Because the more you see, the weaker it gets.

And the next time you find yourself at the first station — the scroll, the glance, the tightening in the chest — you'll know exactly where you are.

You're on the loop. And now you can see it.

Chapter 3: Real Hunger vs. Craving

You got what you wanted.

The notification arrived. The compliment landed. The "yes" came through. Someone saw you, approved of you, validated you. And for a moment — maybe thirty seconds, maybe five minutes — you felt it. The warmth. The relief. The quiet sense of okay, I'm okay now.

And then it faded.

Not because anything bad happened. Not because someone took it back. It just... dissolved. The warmth cooled. The relief thinned. And within the hour, the hollowness was back. The same quiet emptiness. The same urge to check again. The same hunger.

I used to think the problem was that I wasn't getting enough approval. That if I could just get more likes, more compliments, more agreement, I'd finally feel full. I was wrong.

The problem was that I was eating sawdust and calling it food.

The Emptiness After the Applause

Let's look at that moment honestly. The moment after the validation arrives.

You post something. You check. The number is higher than it was. Someone left a nice comment. Your chest loosens. You breathe a little easier. I'm okay, you think. People like me. I'm enough.

And then, thirty minutes later, you're checking again. Not because you're curious. Not because you forgot something. Because the feeling is gone. The reassurance has worn off. The hollowness is back.

Here's the question: if approval were truly feeding you, why does the hunger return so quickly?

Think about real food. A real meal. You eat it, and you're full. Not for five minutes. For hours. The hunger is gone, and it stays gone. Your body has been nourished. It doesn't need to eat again immediately, because the food actually did something.

Now think about approval. The like arrives. The compliment lands. The validation comes through. And for a few minutes, you feel something that resembles fullness. But it doesn't last. It never lasts. Thirty minutes later, the hunger is back. Sometimes it's stronger than before.

So what exactly were you eating?

Not food. Something else. Something that fills the stomach but gives the body nothing it can use. Something that mimics the feeling of being fed without providing any actual nourishment.

But before we talk about what the substitute is, we need to talk about what you're actually hungry for. Because you can't see the difference between sawdust and real food until you know what real food actually is.

What You're Actually Hungry For

You might think you already know the answer. I just want people to like me. That's all. That's what I'm hungry for.

But let's look deeper.

Think about the moments when the hunger is strongest. Not the craving — the deep, quiet hunger underneath it. What is it actually asking for?

It's asking to say what you actually think without editing it first. To speak your real opinion in a room full of people and not immediately soften it, explain it, or apologize for it. It's asking to sit at a dinner table and not perform. To be present, fully present, without monitoring everyone's reactions, without managing their moods, without adjusting your tone to make sure no one is uncomfortable.

It's asking to trust your own judgment. To make a decision without seeking a second opinion. To know, deep down, that your preferences are valid, your boundaries are reasonable, and your instincts are reliable — without needing anyone else to confirm it.

It's asking to feel at peace in your own skin. To exist without the constant, low-level vigilance of self-surveillance. To be in a room and not wonder how you're being perceived. To just... be.

And it's asking to be accepted. Not as the performance. Not as the costume. As you. The actual you. The one who has opinions and preferences and bad days and weird jokes and sometimes doesn't want to go to the party. The one who says "no" sometimes and means it. The one who doesn't always agree. The one who isn't always agreeable.

That's what you're hungry for. And here's the thing: none of that has ever been fed by a like.

So what have you been eating?
