# Chapter 13: I Just Need More Confidence, Right?

You might be thinking something right now.

*I get it. I see the trap. I understand the loop, the Parasite, the Magic Trick, the rigged ruler. All of it. But surely there's something I can do. A technique. A script. A confidence hack. If I just had more self-esteem, I'd be able to say no. If I just knew the right words, I'd finally be free.*

That's a natural thought. After all this dismantling, the mind wants something to build. Something to do. Something to hold onto. But notice what's happening: the reach for a tool is the same reflex the book has been exposing.

I had the same thought. For years, I collected scripts and hacks and confidence tricks. I read the books. I listened to the podcasts. I practiced the techniques. They all failed. Not because I wasn't trying hard enough. Because they were aimed at the wrong target.

## The Reach for a Fix

When the tightening arrives, or the "yes" comes out automatically, what's the first thought?

*I need to fix this. I need a better script. I need more confidence.*

The self-help shelf in the mind lights up. The boundary-setting book. The confidence podcast. The assertiveness course. All promising that the right tool will solve the problem. That if you just learn the technique, memorize the script, build the skill — you'll finally be able to say no without anxiety, speak your truth without fear, stop caring what people think.

And here's the thing: those tools feel good. They feel like progress. They feel like you're doing something. But let me ask you a question.

If someone believes the earth is flat, will a better map fix the problem?

No. The map isn't the issue. The belief is.

If someone believes their worth depends on others' opinions, will a better script fix it?

No. The script isn't the issue. The belief is.

The reach for a fix is aimed at the wrong target. The problem isn't a lack of tools. It's a false belief. And false beliefs can't be fixed with technique.

## The Script Illusion

*But scripts work,* you might be thinking. *I've seen them work. People use boundary scripts and they actually say no.*

Let's look at what actually happens when you use a script.

You memorize the perfect way to say no. You rehearse it in your head. You prepare yourself for the moment. And then the request comes — and the old "yes" comes out anyway. The script vanished the moment the fear arrived.

Or maybe the script works. You say the words. You deliver the no. And then... the anxiety doesn't go away. You said no, but now you're replaying it for three hours, wondering if you sounded rude. Wondering if they're upset. Wondering if you've damaged the relationship.

The script changed your words. But did it change what you believe?

The fear of disapproval is still there. The belief that their opinion determines your worth is still there. The Parasite is still whispering. So what did the script actually fix?

Nothing. It just papered over the crack.

The surface behavior changed, but the underlying belief remained untouched. And as long as the belief is there, the fear will keep coming back. You'll keep needing new scripts. New techniques. New hacks. Because none of them address the actual problem.

## The Confidence Hack Illusion

*If I just felt more confident,* you might say, *I wouldn't care what people think. I need to build my self-esteem.*

Let's look at the confidence hack in action.

The affirmations in the mirror. The power poses. The "fake it till you make it" strategy. You tell yourself *I am confident. I am enough. I don't need anyone's approval.* And for a moment, it feels like something is working.

But then the tightening still arrives when a peer posts a win. The dread still comes when the message goes unanswered. The performance still exhausts you. The confidence hacks didn't remove the fear — they just added a layer of performance on top of it.

Now you're performing confidence while still being crushed by the same false belief underneath. You've added another costume to the wardrobe. The original costume was people-pleasing. The new costume is confidence. But underneath both, the same fear is running.

What is confidence, really?

It's the natural byproduct of trusting yourself. When you trust your judgment, trust your boundaries, trust your worth — confidence follows. It's not something you can fake. It's something that emerges when self-trust is present.

Can you fake self-trust? Can you affirm your way into believing something your actions keep denying?

The confidence hacks don't build self-trust. They just add another costume to the performance. And the performance is the problem, not the solution.

## Another Face of the Big Monster

*But improving my skills is good,* you might be thinking. *Learning is good. What's wrong with wanting to get better?*

Nothing. Learning is good. Growth is good. But there's a difference between genuine learning and the compulsive pursuit of validation-management skills.

Genuine learning says: *I want to communicate more clearly. I want to understand myself better. I want to grow as a person.*

The Skills Illusion says: *I'm deficient. I need to fix myself. If I had the right tools, I'd finally be acceptable.*

The first is growth. The second is self-punishment wearing a growth costume.

Are you learning because you're curious and growing? Or are you learning because you believe you're not enough without the new skill?

The first is legitimate. The second is the Skills Illusion — another face of the Big Monster, whispering that your worth depends on being better than you are.

And here's the trap: the more you pursue skills to fix the feeling of deficiency, the more you reinforce the belief that you're deficient. The pursuit itself is evidence of the false belief. The reaching for tools is the Big Monster's way of saying: *See? You're not enough. You need to be fixed.*

The Skills Illusion isn't about growth. It's about deficiency. And the deficiency was never real.

## What Actually Changes the Game

So if scripts don't work, and hacks don't work, and confidence tricks don't work — what does?

Let me ask you something.

What changed when you turned on the light and saw the coat on the chair?

You were afraid. Your heart was pounding. Your breath was quick. And then the light came on, and you saw that the monster was just a coat. And the fear — the physical fear, the pounding heart, the quickened breath — vanished.

Instantly. Not gradually. Not through effort.

Did you need a technique to stop being afraid? A script? A confidence hack?

No. The fear vanished because the belief vanished. The change wasn't a new skill. It was a new understanding.

That's what this book is doing. Not giving you tools. Giving you sight.

When the false belief dissolves, the behavior changes naturally. Not because you learned a script. Because you no longer believe the story that made the old behavior necessary.

When you see that the tide can't be controlled, you stop exhausting yourself trying to hold it back. When you see that the ruler is rigged, you stop trusting its verdicts. When you see that people-pleasing isn't kindness, the compulsion loses its moral cover.



You don't need more confidence. You need more clarity. The false belief is the problem. And once it dissolves, the behavior changes on its own — not because you learned a script, but because you no longer believe the story that made the old behavior necessary.

So the problem isn't a skills deficit — it's a false belief.

But before we move to the reframe, let's take stock of what the performance has actually cost you. Across all three clusters — the validation hunt, the people-pleasing, the comparison game — the toll has been accumulating silently for years.

That's the next chapter. And it's where the full price of the Applause Trap becomes undeniable.

For now? Keep watching. Keep noticing the "I need to fix this" impulse when it arises. Keep observing the reach for a script, a hack, a technique.

Don't try to stop the reaching. Just see it for what it is: the Big Monster, whispering that you're not enough as you are.

And you are.
