3: Real Hunger vs. Craving

You got what you wanted.

The notification arrived. The compliment landed. The "yes" came through. Someone saw you, approved of you, validated you. And for a moment — maybe thirty seconds, maybe five minutes — you felt it. The warmth. The relief. The quiet sense of *okay, I'm okay now*.

And then it faded.

Not because anything bad happened. Not because someone took it back. It just... dissolved. The warmth cooled. The relief thinned. And within the hour, the hollowness was back. The same quiet emptiness. The same urge to check again. The same hunger.

I used to think the problem was that I wasn't getting enough approval. That if I could just get more likes, more compliments, more agreement, I'd finally feel full. I was wrong.

The problem was that I was eating sawdust and calling it food.

## The Emptiness After the Applause

Let's look at that moment honestly. The moment after the validation arrives.

You post something. You check. The number is higher than it was. Someone left a nice comment. Your chest loosens. You breathe a little easier. *I'm okay,* you think. *People like me. I'm enough.*

And then, thirty minutes later, you're checking again. Not because you're curious. Not because you forgot something. Because the feeling is gone. The reassurance has worn off. The hollowness is back.

Here's the question: if approval were truly feeding you, why does the hunger return so quickly?

Think about real food. A real meal. You eat it, and you're full. Not for five minutes. For hours. The hunger is gone, and it stays gone. Your body has been nourished. It doesn't need to eat again immediately, because the food actually did something.

Now think about approval. The like arrives. The compliment lands. The validation comes through. And for a few minutes, you feel something that resembles fullness. But it doesn't last. It never lasts. Thirty minutes later, the hunger is back. Sometimes it's stronger than before.

So what exactly were you eating?

Not food. Something else. Something that fills the stomach but gives the body nothing it can use. Something that mimics the feeling of being fed without providing any actual nourishment.

But before we talk about what the substitute is, we need to talk about what you're actually hungry for. Because you can't see the difference between sawdust and food until you know what real food is.

## What You're Actually Hungry For

You might think you already know the answer. *I just want people to like me. That's all. That's what I'm hungry for.*

But let's look deeper.

Think about the moments when the hunger is strongest. Not the craving — the deep, quiet hunger underneath it. What is it actually asking for?

It's asking to say what you actually think without editing it first. To speak your real opinion in a room full of people and not immediately soften it, explain it, or apologize for it. It's asking to sit at a dinner table and not perform. To be present, fully present, without monitoring everyone's reactions, without managing their moods, without adjusting your tone to make sure no one is uncomfortable.

It's asking to trust your own judgment. To make a decision without seeking a second opinion. To know, deep down, that your preferences are valid, your boundaries are reasonable, and your instincts are reliable — without needing anyone else to confirm it.

It's asking to feel at peace in your own skin. To exist without the constant, low-level vigilance of self-surveillance. To be in a room and not wonder how you're being perceived. To just... be.

And it's asking to be accepted. Not as the performance. Not as the costume. As you. The actual you. The one who has opinions and preferences and bad days and weird jokes and sometimes doesn't want to go to the party. The one who says "no" sometimes and means it. The one who doesn't always agree. The one who isn't always agreeable.

That's what you're hungry for. And here's the thing: none of that has ever been fed by a like.

None of it has been fed by a head-nod. None of it has been fed by a surface compliment. None of it has been fed by performative agreement. Not once. Not ever.

So what have you been eating?

## The Five Real Hungers

Let's name them clearly. Because once you can name them, you can start to see how little the applause has been doing.

**Belonging.** The desire to be welcomed, included, and valued. Not as a performer, but as a person. Not because you're agreeable, helpful, or easy to be around — but because you're you. The hunger to be part of something without having to earn your place in it every single moment.

**Self-Trust.** The desire to know that your own judgment is reliable. That your preferences matter. That your boundaries are reasonable. The hunger to make a decision and not immediately wonder if you've made the wrong one. To know that even if you make a mistake, you can handle it — without needing someone else to tell you it's okay.

**Authentic Expression.** The desire to speak, act, and live in alignment with who you actually are. Not the edited version. Not the costume. The hunger to say what you mean and mean what you say, without calculating how it will land first.

**Peaceful Presence.** The desire to feel calm, grounded, and at ease in your own skin. Without the constant vigilance. Without the self-surveillance. The hunger to exist without performing. To sit in silence without feeling like you need to fill it. To be seen without managing what people see.

**Respect.** The desire to be treated with basic dignity and consideration. Not conditional on agreement. Not conditional on compliance. Not conditional on being pleasant or helpful or easy. The hunger to be valued even when you say no. Even when you disagree. Even when you're inconvenient.

Now ask yourself this: which of these hungers has ever been satisfied by a like?

Which one has been fed by a notification? Which one has been nourished by a surface compliment? Which one has been met by the brief, warm flutter of someone agreeing with you?

The answer is none. Not one.

The applause was never aimed at these hungers. It was aimed at something else entirely. Something that looked like food but wasn't.

## The Craving Is a Substitute

Here's the metaphor that changed everything for me. And I think it might change something for you too.

Imagine you're starving. Truly, deeply hungry. You haven't eaten real food in days. Your body is desperate for nourishment. You can feel the hunger in your bones.

And then someone hands you a bowl of sawdust.

You eat it. Of course you eat it. You're starving. And for a moment — a brief, deceptive moment — your stomach feels full. The hunger pangs subside. The gnawing quiets down. You feel something that resembles satisfaction.

But your body is not nourished. The sawdust gives you nothing. No vitamins. No minerals. No energy. Nothing your body can actually use. And an hour later, you're hungrier than before. Because now you're starving *and* your stomach is full of something worthless.

That's what external validation is. It's sawdust.

The like, the compliment, the head-nod, the performative agreement — it fills the stomach for a moment. It quiets the hunger pangs. It feels like eating. But it gives you nothing. No belonging. No self-trust. No authentic expression. No peaceful presence. No respect. Just a temporary, hollow fullness that fades within the hour.

And the relief you feel when the validation arrives? That relief is real. I'm not telling you it's imaginary. But it's not the relief of being fed. It's the relief of being temporarily distracted from hunger.

The sawdust doesn't feed you. It just makes the hunger quieter for a moment. And then the hunger returns, louder than before.

## Why the Substitute Leaves You Emptier

You might be thinking something right now. Something like: *At least the validation does something. Even if it's temporary, it's better than nothing.*

But here's the problem. The sawdust isn't just neutral. It's not just inadequate. It's actively harmful.

Think about it. You're starving. You eat a bowl of sawdust. Your stomach feels full for a moment, so you stop looking for real food. You think you've eaten. You think you're satisfied. But your body is still starving. It's still desperate for nourishment. And now it's also exhausted from trying to digest something worthless.

An hour later, the hunger returns — but it's worse than before. Because now you've not only failed to feed the real hunger, you've spent energy chasing a substitute. You've delayed the search for real food. You've convinced yourself, temporarily, that you're fine — when you're not.

That's what the applause does. It's not just that it fails to satisfy the real hunger. It's that it *deepens* the hunger it pretends to satisfy.

Look at the cycle. The first like feels good. The tenth like feels okay. The hundredth like feels like nothing. The thousandth like feels like a number. Each one delivers less than the one before, because each one is doing nothing for you. And yet the craving keeps escalating. The checking gets more frequent. The posting gets more strategic. The performance gets more exhausting.

Because the real hunger isn't being fed. It's growing. And the sawdust is just making it worse.

That's why the loop from the last chapter accelerates. That's why the comparison gets sharper, the inferiority gets deeper, the validation-seeking gets more desperate, the people-pleasing gets more exhausting. The whole machine is running faster and faster, chasing a substitute that can never satisfy it. And the more it chases, the emptier it gets.

## The Magic Trick Connection

Now, you might be thinking: *But the craving is real. I feel it. It's so intense. It must be real hunger. It must be pointing toward something I actually need.*

Let me be clear: the craving is real. The physical sensation is real. The urgency is real. When you feel the phantom urge to check your phone, the four-edit text rewrite, the automatic "yes" — that's not imaginary. It's happening in your body.

But intensity is not evidence of legitimacy.

If you were starving and someone offered you sawdust, the craving for it might feel overwhelming. Your body would be desperate for anything that looked like food. The urgency would be real. The pull would be powerful.

But would the intensity of the craving change what sawdust is? Would it make it nourishing? No. It would still be sawdust.

The craving is real. The substitute is still a substitute.

This is the Magic Trick again — the same one we saw in the loop. The craving feels like proof that you need approval. It feels like evidence that validation is what you're hungry for. But the craving was manufactured by the habit itself. The habit created the hunger — or rather, the habit *exploited* a real hunger and pointed it toward a fake solution.

You are truly hungry. That part is real. The belonging, the self-trust, the authentic expression, the peaceful presence, the respect — those are legitimate needs. They're real food. And you've been starving for them.

But the approval? The validation? The applause? That's the sawdust. It's been marketed to you as food, but it isn't food. And the intensity of your craving for it is not proof that it's what you need. It's proof of how deeply you've been starving — and how thoroughly the substitute has been sold to you as the solution.

## What Real Food Looks Like

So if approval is sawdust, what is real food? And how do you get it?

I'm not going to give you a five-step plan. I'm not going to hand you a list of strategies. That's not what this book is. But I can point you toward something.

Think about the moments in your life when the hunger was actually quiet. Not because you'd just received validation. Because you hadn't needed any.

The conversation where you said exactly what you meant, and nothing bad happened. The time you said "no" to something you didn't want to do, and the world didn't end. The moment you trusted your own judgment, made a decision, and it turned out fine. The evening where you sat with people and didn't perform — and it felt easier than you expected.

These moments exist. They're rare, maybe. But they're real. And they point toward something important.

What if the real food isn't something you have to chase at all? What if it's already available — once you stop filling up on sawdust?

The hunger you're feeling is not a sign that something is wrong with you. It's not evidence that you're broken, deficient, or inadequate. It's evidence that you've been eating the wrong thing. The hunger is healthy. It's pointing toward real needs. Real food. Real nourishment.

And once the sawdust stops — once you stop reaching for the approval, the validation, the applause — the real food becomes visible. The belonging you actually crave. The self-trust you actually need. The authentic expression you actually hunger for. The peaceful presence that's been buried under the performance. The respect that comes from being real, not from being agreeable.

That's where this book is going. But we're not there yet. Because before we can talk about what happens when the sawdust stops, we need to understand the sawdust itself more clearly. Specifically, we need to understand why the craving feels so physical. Why your chest tightens when a message goes unanswered. Why your stomach hollows out when the engagement plateaus. Why your heart quickens when someone disagrees.

Because the craving has two parts. A physical flicker and a mental story.

And one of them is much, much smaller than the other.

That's the next chapter. For now? Keep watching. Keep noticing the difference between what you're craving and what you're actually hungry for. Don't try to change anything. Just observe.

The sawdust is about to be explained.
